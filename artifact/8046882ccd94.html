A SIM card you can program.

Programmatic telephony is gated behind business registration everywhere — Twilio, Telnyx, and the regional CPaaS alike. CallAgent gives an individual the same thing from a USB LTE modem and a SIM they already own, with the call audio never leaving their machine.

What it does

A Quectel EG21-G modem plugs into USB and presents two things at once: an AT-command control channel and a USB Audio Class sound card. CallAgent drives both — it places and answers real calls, streams the 8 kHz call audio through speech recognition, an LLM, and speech synthesis, and puts the reply back on the line fast enough to hold a conversation. Barge-in works: talk over the agent and it stops.

The parts that were actually hard

USB
macOS won't expose the modem's interfaces as serial ttys, so AT control talks directly to USB bulk endpoints via libusb — interface 2 of an eight-interface composite device. Matching on vendor ID alone grabs the wrong Quectel; it takes VID and PID.
Real-time
Control and audio are separate transports that have to stay in sync. Getting PCM registration ordered correctly around answer and hangup was the difference between a working call and a wedged engine that killed the next one too.
Inference
Porting the speech stack to Rust meant reverse-engineering IndicConformer's log-mel featurizer to run the model on pure ONNX Runtime — no PyTorch, no Python sidecar, no 2 GB dependency in a desktop installer.
Judgment
The server was specced in Go and I reversed the decision to Python once the speech stack existed: CGo negated Go's single-binary win, and local GPU inference had no Go equivalent. The reasoning is written down in the repo, tradeoff included.

Why it's going open source

I started this to solve my own problem — I wanted my phone number to be programmable and found out I wasn't allowed to have that. Then I kept meeting people with the same problem: someone who wants an agent to screen their calls, take a message while they're driving, or send a reminder SMS, and who owns a number they simply can't program.

That isn't a niche, it's a locked door with a lot of people behind it — and the honest answer is that it gets opened faster in the open than by me alone. The hardware layer especially: every modem is its own dialect of AT commands, and no one person owns enough of them to write the driver everyone needs. So the plan is to open the repos, publish the hard-won hardware notes, and build the project around the people who show up.
